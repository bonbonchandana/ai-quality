    Exact steps for this dataset
      Use your own data

      Upload an hourly CSV, Excel or ZIP file with a time column (Date + Time, year/month/day/hour or datetime) and at least one pollutant column (CO, NO2, NOx, C6H6, PM2.5, PM10, SO2 or O3). Weather columns are used if present. The UCI Beijing station files work as they are.

      For developers: try the API (POST /predict)

      The same request Postman sends. "Load a real hour" fills the boxes in from the test period.

      How it works, in four steps

      1. 1
        Collect the data

        For the chosen place we get the hourly levels of six pollutants (fine dust, coarse dust, nitrogen dioxide, ozone, sulphur dioxide and carbon monoxide) and the weather, going back three months.

      2. 2
        Learn the patterns

        The model studies how pollution usually changes from one hour to the next: the last few hours, the same hour yesterday, the time of day and the weather. We try a couple of methods and keep the one that does best on days it hasn't seen.

      3. 3
        Forecast the next 24 hours

        Starting from the latest reading, the model predicts the next hour, then uses that to predict the hour after, and so on. The shaded range shows how far off it has usually been on recent days.

      4. 4
        Spot unusual hours

        When the real level is much further from what the model expected than usual, we check why. If other pollutants jumped at the same time, it was probably a real pollution event. If readings were missing, stuck or jumped on their own, it was probably a data problem. Otherwise we say we're not sure.

      Words you'll see

      AQI (air quality index)
      One number from 0 to 500 that sums up how polluted the air is. Under 50 is good; over 150 is unhealthy for everyone. We use the US EPA scale, based on fine dust.
      PM2.5 (fine dust)
      Particles smaller than 2.5 micrometres, about 30 times thinner than a hair. They get deep into the lungs, so they matter most for health. Measured in µg/m³ (micrograms in a cubic metre of air).
      Forecast range
      The shaded band around the forecast. On recent days the real value landed inside it about 8 times out of 10.
      Typical miss
      How far our prediction usually is from what really happened. Smaller is better.
      Simple guess
      Assuming the next hour will be exactly the same as now. A forecast is only worth using if it beats this.
      Unusual hour
      An hour when the real level was further from our forecast than on 95% of normal hours.

      Where the data comes from

      Live data comes from Open-Meteo, which shares the CAMS air quality model run by the European Copernicus programme. It estimates pollution everywhere on Earth on a grid of about 11 km, so it shows the general air in your area rather than one street. The research datasets come from the UCI Machine Learning Repository. Nothing here needs an account or a key.

      This is a forecasting tool, not medical advice. For official warnings, follow your local pollution control board.
